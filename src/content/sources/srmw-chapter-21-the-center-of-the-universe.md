<p>“I’m sorry,” I said. “But I don’t know who you are either.”</p>

<p>“You’ve heard of the Big Bang, of course,” said Lemaître.</p>

<p>“Yes,” I said. “I think most knowledgeable people have heard of it.</p>

<p>“And does anyone who has read Prologue 2, <em>And God Said: “Let There Be Light! Dammit!”</em> Added the metanovel.</p>

<p>“And you know about the cosmic background microwave radiation?” Lemaître asked.</p>

<p>“Well, I know a little bit,” I answered. “I know that when you point a radio telescope to the sky you see the same radiation in every direction.”</p>

<p>“That’s approximately correct,” said Lemaître. “We see a thermal black body spectrum at temperature 2.725 Kelvin, which peaks at the microwave frequency of 160.2 GHz, corresponding to a 1.873 mm wavelength.”</p>

<p>“I understand all of those words,” I said, “And I read the article you’re quoting in the Wikipedia. But the importance is beyond me.”</p>

<p>“That’s alright,” said Lemaître. “What’s important is that it’s a very faint glow which is the same in all directions.”</p>

<p>“I understand that,” I said.</p>

<p>“Penzias and Wilson discovered it,” Lemaître added. “They got a Nobel Prize for their work.”</p>

<p>“I didn’t know that, either,” I said.</p>

<p>“They did,” said Lemaître. “And it was important confirming evidence for the Big Bang. Do you understand why?”</p>

<p>“No,” I said. “I knew there was a connection, but I’m not sure what it was.”</p>

<p>Actually I did know the connection. The Wikipedia articles that I had read, researching this chapter, had explained it. But dialog was better than a lecture would have been.</p>

<p>“Both boring,” said my inner critic.</p>

<p>“You remember that the further out in space an object is, the longer it must have taken for its photons to get to you?” Asked Penzias.</p>

<p>“Yes,” I said.</p>

<p>“So when you look far out in space you’re actually looking back in time.”</p>

<p>“Yes,” I said. “And I remember that the red-shift tells us how far away an object is, so we know how long ago it emitted its light.”</p>

<p>“That’s correct. The oldest confirmed galaxy, UDFj-39546284, is on the order of 13.2 billion light-years away—or 13.2 billion years in the past. More than billion years after the Big Bang. The background radiation is even older than that,” said Penzias.</p>

<p>“The background radiation comes from the furthest detectable boundary of our universe,” said Wilson.</p>

<p><span class="pg" id="page-167" data-page="167" aria-hidden="true"></span>“And since the boundary is the same in every direction, it follows that we cannot be nearer any part of the boundary than any other,” said Lemaître.</p>

<p>I thought about that for a while.</p>

<p>“That makes sense,” I said finally. “If the Big Bang Theory explains the origin of the universe, then the universe must have expanded from a point. So if you go back far enough in time, you must be at that point.”</p>

<p>“Correct,” said Penzias. “And since this is true for every point in the universe, it means...”</p>

<p>“It means that wherever you are you’re at the center of the universe,” I said. I thought about that for a moment. “It means that everyone is at the center of the universe.”</p>

<p>I looked around me. I imagined perceiving the space in the room around me. I imagined expanding my consciousness until I perceived—however dimly—the entire universe around me. And I was in the center. It was a great feeling.</p>

<p>“Wow!” I said. “I am at the center! And so is everyone. That’s just incredible.”</p>

<p>“If I may offer an opinion, both as a physicist, and as a Catholic priest,” said Monseigneur Lemaître, “God is a fucking genius.”</p>
