“Brilliant!” I said to myself, once again.
Now that I’d conceived of writing a self-referential metanovel, I needed characters and a
plot.
“I’m a character in my metanovel, of course,” I said, “And I can make the Not Quite Omni­
scient Narrator a character!” I said. The Not Quite Omniscient Narrator knew it was a narrator,
but had not been quite sure it would be a character as well when it had started narrating. Now it
knew. And it knew it would become omniscient. But it still wasn’t quite sure when God would
grant its wish.
“And you readers? You. You can be characters, too.”
“You mean me, the reader reading this sentence?” A reader asked.
“Or do you mean me? The one reading this sentence?” Asked another.
“Or me?” Asked others.
“All of you,” I answered. “If the Not Quite Omniscient Narrator and I can be characters, so
can every one of you.”
“Brilliant!” I said to myself again.
“And God!” I continued. “God created this metauniverse. She can be a character too!”
“Great,” muttered my inner critic.
“And then there’s my inner critic. You can be another character.”
“Great,” muttered my inner critic. “Now I can’t avoid contributing to this stupidity.”
“And my ideas. They can be characters,” I said. “I can make anything a character.”
“Great,” muttered my inner critic.
“Even me!” Said a piece of household hardware that would later be in the metanovel, but
could not be named now without creating an inconsistency later.
“The story will be me writing about writing this novel,” I said, “but that might become bor­
ing. I’ll need to create some other kind of a problem. A conflict. Maybe I’ll invent a character
who tries to keep me from finishing it.
The Not Quite Omniscient Narrator knew many things that the author did not. It knew that
Freetown was the capital of Sierra Leone, that the standard atomic weight of lawrencium was
