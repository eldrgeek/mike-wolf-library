Prologue 1: Improbability
Here you are, an improbable person, reading an improbable novel, because an improbable
author, improbably wrote it. And even more improbably, you are reading this very sentence at
this very moment, the moment this book predicts you will read it!
Think about the improbability!
For 14.75 billion years you didn’t exist. For all practical purposes, you were dead. You were
billions of atoms, spread across the vastness of space. But now, here you are! You exist. You
think, therefore you are.
For 14.75 billion years the author of this book also didn’t exist. Like you, for all practical
purposes, the author was dead. Like you he was billions of atoms, spread across the vastness of
space. Yet here he is writing this sentence. And this one. He writes, therefore he is.
For 14.75 billion years the book you are reading didn’t exist. You could have existed, and
the author could have existed and still this book might not have existed. Yet it exists. The author
writes it and you read it, therefore it is.
Now consider: What are the chances that you would exist, that the author would exist, that
the book would exist, and that you would be reading yet another sentence at precisely the pre­
dicted moment—the moment when you are reading about reading it?
Think about it!
The joint improbabilities are so large that it’s nearly impossible that the entire constellation
of events could ever have taken place. Yet it has. Or, if you speak English rather than American,
they have.
Is it just a coincidence? Or is it merely a strong corollary of the weak anthropic principle—
that only in a universe capable of supporting living beings intelligent enough to write and read a
self-referential metanovel that considers the improbability of living beings intelligent enough to
write and read a self-referential metanovel that observes its own improbability could such a
question be asked?
Or is there a deeper meaning?
The assembly of atoms that is you is on its way to the heat death of the universe—which is
conveniently described on page 86. That is your assembly’s destiny, and therefore it is ultimately
yours.
But in the meantime, things can happen. There are questions that can be answered. Among
them are these:
Will you keep reading this metanovel?
Will you learn about God’s gifts to you, including, but not limited to: free will, evolution, the
scientific method, and the self-referential metanovel?
Will you learn about the most powerful forces in the universe?
Will you learn to harness them?
Will you learn why you might want to write your own metanovel?
Will you learn how?
Will you start writing your own?

Novels and metanovels follow inviolable rules. A novel must take place in a universe. A
metanovel must take place in a metauniverse. Until a metauniverse exists, rules limit what a
metanovel’s author can write. This list is fairly boring, as is this paragraph.
A metanovel author can can write apologies and disclaimers, can explain improbabilities,
can have God speak, can have an inner critic criticize. Beyond that there’s little else a metanovel
author can do. It gets boring for the author and boring for the readers. Like it is right now. Bor­
ing. Boring. Boring.
A metanovel author has just one way to relieve the boredom: write a prologue in which a
metauniverse is created. Then anything goes. The rules say that such a prologue must begin with
two initial paragraphs, much like the paragraphs above, that explain the existence of rules, the
need for a metauniverse, why very little is happening, and most of the things that can happen.
Such paragraphs are boring, and must acknowledge that. They must be followed by a paragraph,
much like this one, that explains the prior paragraphs, explains itself, and explains that the pro­
logue in which it is contained can have more paragraphs, but if and only if these paragraphs de­
scribe the creation of a metauniverse, and if and only if the very next paragraph begins with
God’s copyrighted words “Let there be Light!©.’ This paragraph is also boring and must so in­
form the reader. As this one now does.
“Let there be Light!” God says, thus beginning the sequence of paragraphs that will culmin­
ate in the creation of the metanovel’s metauniverse. Things can become interesting, but first the
author must shift to the past tense because the creation of a metauniverse must logically precede
the prologue explaining it. Changing tenses provides tension, which momentarily replaces bore­
dom with an extremely lame pun. Boredom was better, but it’s too late now. We resume the ac­
tion with an instant replay of God’s words, transitioning to the proper tense:
