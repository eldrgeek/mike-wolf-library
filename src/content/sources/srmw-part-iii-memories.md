“Michael! Darling! You look wonderful!” Her Brooklyn accent and eighty years of heavy ci­

“Mom!” I said, surprised. “What are you doing in my metanovel? You’re supposed to be

“I guess so,” I said. We’d made her smoke outside the house while she was alive, but it

She reached into her battered purse, rummaged around and pulled out a pack of Camels and
a Bic lighter. She pulled out a cigarette—her hands were still shaking, death hadn’t helped the
tremor—and handed me the lighter. I lit her cigarette. She took a healthy drag.
“Dad says hello,” she said and she blew out the smoke. “He’s playing bridge. Maybe he’ll
come by later.”
She looked around. “Page 53!” She said. “Very nice! I always knew you’d write a book
some day. It’s really too bad you didn’t write it while Dad was alive.”
“I’ve written a lot more than 53 pages,” I said. “They come later. And you’ll be in it later. In
fact,” I checked the last draft, “the last page is 216.”
“Wonderful, darling, wonderful.” She looked around. “Can I smoke here?”
“You’re already smoking,” I said. “Let me get you an ashtray.” Her ashes were dropping on
the page.

In the Beginning
Before the beginning, his beginning, half of what was to become him was a fast-swimming,
determined, spermatozoon from his father’s recent ejaculate; the other half was the ovum that his
mother had ripened from an oogonium just in time to meet that plucky, lucky, tail-lashing
sperm. His two halves joined and he became a human zygote—Zygote 0.0 in the zygote number­
ing system he later invented to amuse himself and annoy his daughters—a single-celled animal
whose ontogeny would recapitulate its phylogeny as it raced for 266 days, at the rate of 165.34
years per second, down the evolutionary trail that his ancestors had taken 3.8 billion years to
blaze.
The little zygote wasted no time. Even as he drifted down his mother’s left fallopian tube on
the long, one-stop journey to the outside world, he began to divide mitotically. After four genera­
tions he reached his first metazooic stage and had become a clump of about sixteen cells known
as a morula. It was the first of the self-transformations that would continue the rest of his life.
He kept dividing, and by the time he had reached eighty cells he had become a blastocyst.
But he didn’t stop. He pressed on through gastrulation, formed a protein shell around himself,
hatched, and implanted himself in his mother’s endrometrium. Now the young gastrula looked
much like the simplest animals—sponges, coral, and jellyfish—but with three cell layers, not
two.
He continued to transform. He became worm-like, then tadpole-like, then grew fish-like gills
and discarded them. His arms and legs budded. He grew a penis. By eight weeks he’d graduated
to fetus He looked like a movie alien, and was en route to becoming a human. And he was con­
tinuing to transform himself.
He evolved and grew until he was a complete human baby able to carry out basic baby func­
tions like breathing, sucking, crying, pooping, and keeping parents awake. Now it was time for
the final leg of his journey: his mother’s powerful uterine muscles would squeeze him through
her now-dilated cervix, down her birth canal, out her vaginal opening, and into the cold, bright
world.

cells. She was inside him, and inside this metanovel from the beginning, and whenever he had
thought he was fighting her, he had really been fighting himself.

Literary Near-Immortality
Kurt Vonnegut had lived both as a human person in the real universe and as a metafictional
character in several of the books he wrote: Breakfast of Champions, Slaughterhouse Five, and
Timequake. Each time Vonnegut projected himself into one of his book he increased his chances
for literary near-immortality.
Vonnegut, the human, died in 2007, but metafictional Vonneguts will live as long as any one
remembers him, or any copy of any medium in which he is a metafictional character exists. An
image of Vonnegut had lived in the author’s memory ever since he read Vonnegut’s book Cat’s
Cradle and continued to live there after Vonnegut’s death.
Because of the nature of the author’s metanovel, the Vonnegut memory was able to push its
way from the back of the author’s mind to the front, to get Vonnegut mentioned in New Chapter
3, called Writers I Admire on page 141. Vonnegut appeared for a one-liner in The Imagined
Meeting on page 143 and the author’s mother talked about him in The Tree Of Life on page 172.
Emboldened, the Vonnegut living within the author helped write and edit this chapter. It
wasn’t as good anything that Vonnegut had written when he was alive, but for a partly dead guy,
it wasn’t bad.
“So it goes,” Kurt Vonnegut said after reading what he’d helped write.. “So it goes.”
Not Quite Omniscient Narrator knows that in the Earth year 214,334 a space explorer,
named Kilgore Trout, after one of Vonnegut’s fictional characters, will spend 20,000 of his ex­
tended life-span years searching for evidence that the Vonnegutian fictional planet Tralfamadore
which exists in five separate Vonnegutian fictional universes: The Sirens of Titan, God Bless
You, Mr. Rosewater, Slaughterhouse-five, Hocus Pocus, and Timequake, also exists in his own
universe.
Trout will accidentally drop a carbonite-encased, nearly indestructible Condensed Library
of Babel Chip on asteroid KB71W91223 as it travels through intergalactic space. The Con­
densed Library of Babel is similar to the Library of Babel—a concept attributed to the Earth au­
thor Jorge Luis Borges by the Earth philosopher Daniel Dennett who introduced this book’s au­
thor to it through Dennett’s book Freedom Evolves, which describes the library as one containing
all possible books. Unlike the full Library of Babel, the Condensed Library is contains only
books that were completed after 2008. the year after Vonnegut’s death and which will have been
completed before the Earth Year 20,552, the year in which the last bit of useful knowledge will
have been discovered by the Jewish Chinese scientist, poet, and talk-show host, Mordecai Fong.
The Condensed Library contains this book, which in turn contains Kurt Vonnegut.
Because of Kilgore Trout’s accident, long after all the last star in the universe burns out,
KB71W91223, with its nearly indestructible library chip will continue its nearly endless and
largely meaningless journey toward the heat death of the universe, giving both Kurt Vonnegut,
this metanovel’s metafictional author, and all the other characters in the metanovel, a nearly end­
less and largely meaningless continued existence.
“So it goes,” said the author to Kurt Vonnegut.
“So it goes,” Vonnegut replied.

Mom, Continued
Mom was funny, creative, adventurous, and utterly fearless. She supported whatever we did
—unless we crossed her. No one who crossed her got away with it. Not Dad. Not us. Cross her?
Watch out!
“That was me?” Asked my mother. “I don’t remember that.”
Everyone loved Dad. He was a sweet, smart man who didn’t seem to mind being the butt of
so many family pranks and jokes. He was stroke stricken at 76, lost mobility and gained speech
problems and a walker. The important parts of his mind stayed intact. The stroke, on top of late
onset diabetes, led to poor circulation in his feet, and ten years later to gangrene. “We have to
amputate,” his doctor told him. “No,” Dad answered. “Then you’re going to die,” his doctor
said. “I was going to die anyway,” Dad said. They didn’t amputate. He did die. But, as my then-
brother-in-law Glen said at Dad’s memorial service, “He went down undefeeted.”
“He did?” Asked my mother. “I don’t remember that.”
We all loved Mom. We all feared her. And for parts of my life I hated her. When I hit my
teens I started reading books on abnormal psychology and learned that she was the reason I was
so fucked up and miserable. I was gracious and I didn’t hold it against her; I understood how
lesser people could make these sorts of mistakes. Generously, I shared the fruits of my research
and patiently explained all the mistakes she’d made with me so she could avoid fucking my sis­
ter up. For some reason this enraged her. And it didn’t help my sister.
“You did that?” Asked my mother. “I don’t remember that.”
From time to time we’d patch things up, then things would fall apart again as our power
struggle continued. As an adult, so-called, I got so angry that I cut my Mom out of my life. Dad
was attached to Mom, so I cut him out, too. Too bad, Dad.
It was hard to understand how the person I knew myself to be—someone who, Christlike,
would have died to save the world if dying wasn’t such a buzz-kill—could have behaved the
way that I did. But he did and I did. I knew that there was something wrong with me. If you’d
read the books on abnormal psychology that I had read, you’d know, as I did, that everything
wrong with me was Mom’s fault.
Because I wasn’t speaking to them, I didn’t tell Mom and Dad when Bobbi became pregnant
with our first daughter, Dana. After she was born, in what I convinced myself was a gesture of
reconciliation, I showed up on their doorstep and presented their first granddaughter. I didn’t say
the words, but my message of reconciliation was clear: “Fuck you, Mom!”
“I don’t remember that,” Mom said. “Did you do that?”
Dad was furious and threw me out. So I left, angry and vindicated, instead of apologizing as

Finally I came to my senses, realized what a jerk I had been, and apologized to them both.
