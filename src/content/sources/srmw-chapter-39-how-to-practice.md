<p>“You heard my talk,” said Barbara Baig. “You’ve got my book in your bookcase. True, it’s about writing practice, but it applies to any kind of practice.”</p>

<p>“I know,” I said, embarrassed. “I didn’t realize it until now. It’s obvious.”</p>

<p>“I told you,” said the doorknob.</p>

<p>“Well, he did finally remember it,” said the metanovel. “That’s something, isn’t it.”</p>

<p>“More blather,” said my inner critic.</p>

<p>“Shut up!” Barbara said to my inner critic. She turned to me. “Remember what I said when you heard me talk at the Blue Hill Library,” she said. “It’s also in my book.”</p>

<p>I pulled out my copy of Barbara’s <em>How to Be a Writer</em> and checked it:</p>

<blockquote class="cite"><p>Everyone accepts that athletes and musicians learn their skills and keep them honed through practice...We accept that if someone is going to undertake a public performance —on an athletic field, or in a concert—she has to build her skills and prepare herself through practice. ...it’s almost impossible to learn skills under performance conditions; ...we’re too focused on the result...</p></blockquote>

<p>“You’re right,” I said. “And you also said that one of the problems with most practice is that it’s focused on the characteristics of the finished product, not the skills needed to produce it.</p>

<p>“Precisely,” Barbara said. “So what are the skills you need?”</p>

<p>I thought for a moment. What did I need to do to get the result that I wanted?</p>

<p>I didn’t have trouble putting words on paper. I’d had enough practice. And I wasn’t having trouble editing any more. I had, in earlier sections of the book, but I’d had enough practice.</p>

<p>I wasn’t good organizing and making plans, but I could do it. But once I had a plan, I was horrible at following through. I would get distracted. I would lose track of time. I would forget that I was trying to follow a plan.</p>

<p>“You think that your distraction due to ADD,” Barbara said. “You think it’s a personality trait.”</p>

<p>“More like character defect,” said my inner critic.</p>

<p>“Didn’t I tell you to shut up?” Asked Barbara. “Now shut up!” She turned back to me.</p>

<p>“There’s no such thing as a personality trait,” said Barbara. “And there’s no such thing as a character defect. They’re not real. They’re imaginary. Don’t make the Third Classic Blunder. What’s real is skill, lack of skill, and degree of skill. That’s all.”</p>

<p>“I already know that practice is a skill,” I said. “What’s missing.”</p>

<p>“It’s so obvious,” said the doorknob.</p>

<p>“The doorknob is right,” Barbara Baig said. “Think about it.”</p>

<p>I thought: <em>Practicing was a skill, but it had component skills,</em> I thought. <em>Perhaps one or more of those skills is missing or weak.</em></p>

<p>I thought some more: <em>To develop a skill, I’ve got to organize my practice. It’s a complex skill, so I’ve got to identify its components. I’ve got to organize time for my practice. And I’ve got to monitor my practice to ensure that it gets done.</em></p>

<p>I thought some more: <em>If something’s wrong I’ve got to take corrective action.</em></p>

<p><span class="pg" id="page-197" data-page="197" aria-hidden="true"></span>I thought more. <em>I’m a practiced thinker,</em> I thought, so I have no trouble thinking.</p>

<p>“I see what I need to do,” I said to Barbara. “I need to structure my practice.”</p>

<p>The author was right, but he’d fail to structure his practice several times before he figured it out.</p>
