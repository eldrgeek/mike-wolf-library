My metanovel was finished.
“Then why are you writing this?” Asked my inner critic. “You just said it was finished.”

“And I learned the importance of having a safe place, and en-joying my life.”
“Didn’t you write it in the first draft?” Asked my inner critic.
“I did,” I said. “Except for the safe place part. That part was really important.”
“And didn’t you write about it more in the fifth, and the twenty-third, and...” my inner critic
listed the drafts during which I’d elaborated on the importance of decision, imagination, belief,
and practice.”
“Yes,” I said. “Except for the safe place part.”
“Then why on earth are you writing it again?” My inner critic asked. “Are you an idiot?”
“There’s a reason,” I answered. “If I keep writing, I’ll know what it is.”
I thought back to November, writing my first draft. Almost all of the ideas that are in this fi­
nal version were in the first draft. But they were buried and disorganized. And they were in my
head even before the first draft. Buried and disorganized.
I knew them, but I didn’t understand them.
“I didn’t practice,” I said. “I knew I should practice but I didn’t do it. Really I didn’t know
how to. What I said about metapractice was true.”
I thought back to November again. Writing the first draft hadn’t been hard. It took a little
time, and it took almost no practice. And it was fun. I found that I cared about what I had writ­
ten. But I couldn’t finish it. I didn’t know how to finish it. I didn’t even know how to edit it.
I’d edit the first five pages, or the first ten, and then quit. I’d try again. And then I’d give up.
I’d get frustrated and put it away for weeks. I’d write new beginnings, and edit them. I’d get
frustrated again. I’d get upset. I’d get angry. I’d get depressed. And finally, I started to practice.
Really practice.
I forced myself to do a complete editing pass, from the front to the back. I threw things out. I
added things. But most of all, I read what I’d written. I paid attention. And I did what I’d been
telling myself to do.
“If you hadn’t been such a moron, you would have done it sooner,” said my inner critic.
“Maybe,” I said. “But in the end I didn’t give up. That’s important to remember, too.”
I did another complete editing pass. And read what I’d written. And applied some more.
I kept doing it.
Practice, Practice Practice!
Then my metanovel came to life. It wanted to be finished.
And then I really started to practice.
I wrote and edited every day.

Metanovel Writing
Self-referential metanovel writing writing is what I used to teach myself what I didn’t know
I needed to learn. Self-referential metanovel writing is what I used to transform myself, and
what I continue to use. Every morning I keep writing my metanovel, my story of myself. And I
spend a lot the rest of the day practicing what I’ve been writing.
You can learn to do it, too.

Metanovel writing is not hard. First, you need to read a self-referential metanovel to gain ex­
perience with one so you can imagine your own.
Then you just have to remember the two fundamental principles of metanovel writing:
First, start writing. Once you start writing a self-referential metanovel, you’re both the
author and a character. That means you can write a change in your character in the
metanovel, then read what you wrote and become that changed character, even if only a
little bit, even if only for a little while. But if you practice writing and reading what
you’ve written you will make the change more complete and more permanent.
That’s all it takes. You just have to start, and to practice.

Joseph Beller stood in the middle of a small room. He’d been dead, but not quite completely

He looked around. The room was nicely furnished and well lit. In one corner was a bed. In

On the desk was a typewriter, and next to the typewriter a stack of paper. On the wall, above
the desk was a bookshelf with just two books. Beller walked over, picked up the first book and
looked at the title. He sat down, stunned. It said: Catch-22 by Joseph Heller.
“Joseph Heller!” He said. He remembered Heller. He’d lived in the apartment across the
hall. He’d wanted to be a writer, and would show Beller his work and ask for criticism. It was
really poor writing.
To his credit, Heller knew he wasn’t a good writer, but he was determined to practice. Some­
how, he told Beller, he was going to publish something.
Beller opened Catch-22 and started reading. It was Catch-33! His Catch-33, practically
word for word. He skipped through it, recognizing familiar passages. He turned to the end. The
last words in the book were the last ones he’d written in his 22nd catch. He shook his head.
He turned to the back cover: critical acclaim! One of the best books of the century.
That fucking muse! He thought. She’d said Catch-33 was worthless.
He picked up the other book. It was Closing Time, also by Joseph Heller. He started reading,
skipped ahead and read some more, skipped ahead and read more. Here were his characters,

hadn’t learned much.
That fucking muse! He thought. That fucking, fucking muse!
He didn’t know where he was. But he knew why he was here. He had another chance. And

Postlude
Self-referential metanovel Writing for D*mmies: a self-referential metanovel is the story of
how Self-referential metanovel Writing for D*mmies: a self-referential metanovel was written—
by me, and the metanovel, with help from my imaginary God.
But it isn’t the whole story.
I wrote pages and pages that never got here. More than a hundred are tacked to the end of
this draft and there are hundreds abandoned in other drafts. They’re all good pages. It just wasn’t
their time.
Every page has at least one idea, and every idea is alive. Each appeared magically, and
taught me something. Some may live in a different book. Some in a blog. Some will be trans­
formed. Others will silently await the heat death of the universe. I am grateful to each.
Writing my self-referential metanovel has helped me do things I’d been trying to do for a
long time. I’m much more organized than I was when I started. I’ve lost ten pounds. I’m exer­
cising regularly.
And I’ve written my first book, and what may well be the world’s first self-referential
metanovel.
Through my story I discovered my safe place and its importance. Now I go there regularly—
in my imagination, at least—and I go whenever I need to. My regular visits help me practice get­
ting there, and since I’m imagining that I’m there, my visits help me practice imagining.
Here’s one way I practice. I set an alarm on my Android that goes off periodically—right
now, every fifteen minutes. When the alarm goes off, the phone vibrates, and I start my session.
I stop what I am doing, shut off and reset the alarm. If possible, I stand up. That’s a good
practice right there, because I spend a lot of time sitting. If I can, I stretch while I’m doing the
rest.
First, I imagine I’m in my safe place, the one that the character who was me in the metanov­
el found. It takes only a second to do that now, because I’ve practiced a lot. Once in a while I de­
vote more than a second, working to making it realer than usual.
I imagine I’m free from any upset, chaos or difficulty in life—mine, or others’. I imagine
that I’m in space, far from Earth, far enough that the nearest thing I can see is a galaxy. I remind
myself that I’m at the center of the universe. I imagine it. I feel it. I remind myself that I’m an
accident and a mistake. I make sure that I feel the creeping seriousness of life sloughing off.
There’s nothing wrong with being serious, providing it’s a useful tool, not a burden. I imagine
that I’m surrounded by family and connected to all of life, wherever it is. I take a moment to feel
the change. Then I take a moment and en-joy the moment. I put as much joy into that moment as
I can in a very short time.
Then I check to make sure I’m on task and haven’t gotten distracted . If I’m not on task, I
stop and make a decision: make what I am doing my new task, or stop, reset, and get back on
task.
Whichever I choose, I now turn my attention to that task. I imagining focusing on my task
and imagine working on it diligently until it’s done, or until the timer goes off again. Then I sit

down, in a brighter, better, more joy filled world than the one I left, and I do what I just ima­

I try to go to my safe place at other times. I’d like to be there all the time—there, completely
safe, looking at the world through my eyes and controlling my mind and body from there.
I don’t go through that whole routine all the time: sometimes because it’s inappropriate, and
sometimes because my practice isn’t perfect. And sometimes, horribly, because I’ve disabled my
alarm, forgotten that I’ve done it, and lost track of time for hours. But most of the time it works.
I’d like to invite you, the reader reading this, to visit my safe place whenever you like.
There’s always room. And it’s easy to get there. Just believe you can; decide to go there; and
imagine that you’re there. If at first you don’t succeed, go meta. And once you get there, Prac­
tice! Practice! Practice!
I imagine that some readers will try that. It’s not hard. If you do drop by, imagine me wel­
coming you. That’s what I imagine I’d do.
If my safe place doesn’t suit you, you can find your own, then practice going there when you
don’t have to, so you can go there when you need to. You can find one by writing a self-referen­
tial metastory in which you find one; or by deciding and imagining. Once you find it: Practice!
Practice! Practice!
It works for me. It might work for you. Or it might not. If it doesn’t then you need to find
your own way. Believe, start, go meta, don’t give up.
You’ll find it.
