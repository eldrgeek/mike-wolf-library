<p>I was four chapters in, and I still had almost no idea of what I was going to write about.</p>

<p>But having almost no idea is like being almost pregnant. The idea was there. I’d conceived something, but I just didn’t quite know what I had conceived.</p>

<p>“Thank you for conceiving me,” said the idea. It was precocious and had developed the power of speech. What I had conceived was becoming an idea.</p>

<p>“You’re quite welcome,” I answered.</p>

<p>“Do you know what I am?” Asked the young idea. “I seem rather vague.”</p>

<p>Then suddenly I realized what I’d conceived. It was brilliant!</p>

<p>“You’re a great idea!” I said, “A brilliant one.”</p>

<p>“I am?” The idea asked.</p>

<p>“I am!” The idea said, as it realized what it was.</p>

<p>“You’re going to star in a chapter of your own,” I said. “The next one.”</p>

<h3 id="a-talking-idea"><span class="pg" id="page-32" data-page="32" aria-hidden="true"></span>A Talking Idea?</h3>

<p>“A talking idea?” Asks my inner critic, rhetorically</p>

<p>“This one talks,” I say.</p>

<p>“And you had a talking idea?” Asks my inner critic, re-torically. “That’s the whole chapter?”</p>

<p>“It’s also about how the idea came to be the one in the metanovel,” I answer. “Not just that I just had it.”</p>

<p>“And that’s it?” Asks my inner critic one more time. “That’s the whole chapter?”</p>

<p>“Pretty much,” I say. “I like it. I think that it creates suspense. Narrative tension. Extra words. The readers are probably wondering ‘What’s the idea?’”</p>

<p>“If there are any readers,” says my inner critic.</p>

<p><em>I’m reading,</em> a reader thought. <em>And I am wondering ‘What’s the idea?’</em></p>
