Reflection 1: Practice! Practice! Practice!
Whatever the author could imagine in the metauniverse could exist in the metauniverse. But
imagining something vaguely made it exist only vaguely. Bringing something substantial into
substantial existence took a substantial amount of imagining. It took work. It took practice.
“I don’t know why I keep forgetting how important practice is,” I said.
“I don’t know why either,” said the metanovel. “You keep writing about it and not doing it.”
Simon Singh’s book, Fermat’s Enigma tells the story of Andrew Wiles, the British mathem­
atician who proved what’s called Fermat’s Last Theorem, a famous mathematical proposition
that had gone unsolved for 358 years. The Pythagorean equation: a2 + b2 = c2 has many integer
solutions. Fermat’s Theorem: no three positive integers can satisfy the equation an + bn = cn for n
greater than two.
The world’s mathematical minds had individually spent years and collectively spent centur­
ies of effort trying to prove the Theorem was true or finding a counterexample. Wiles produced a
400 page proof after six years of intense practice. He would master a branch of mathematics by
reading the literature, working through the theorems, and then by practice. Practice! Practice!
Practice! He’d find problems posed by others; he’d invent problems of his own. He’d practice,
and practice, and practice, until solving problems in that branch of mathematics was as effortless
as adding single digits is to most of us. Then he’d go on to the next branch, practicing, and prac­
ticing, and practicing it until he mastered it.
I was in my fifties when I first read the book and realized what I had failed to realize earlier
and kept forgetting later. Practice was vital. I needed to practice. I had been smart enough to
master most of my schoolwork with almost no effort. Out of school I learned things easily. Not
practicing had became my practice.
Research in neurobiology and cognitive science confirms the need for practice. When people
—or animals—learn, their brains rewire. The more practice the more rewiring. Skills come from
deliberate, intentional repeated practice. The more intention applied, the less practice is needed,
but some practice always is needed. Once skills are acquired, practice sustains skills. Once prac­
tice stops, skills deteriorate. Every new bit of neurological and psychological research says the
same thing: Practice! Practice! Practice!
NaNoWriMo forced me to practice writing. I had only thirty days. I had to practice every
day—1,666 words a day on average. What I wrote wasn’t important. That I practiced was im­
portant.
Then it came time to edit. And I got in trouble.
It might have been easier if I realized that I needed to practice editing.
But I didn’t. Instead I practiced giving up, starting over, and taking long absences from even
trying.
And because I practiced it, I got good at it.
Because of my childhood successes I’d not only learned to not practice, I’d also not learned
to not metapractice: to practice practicing.
I needed to change that.

Reflection 4: Imagining The Metanovel
I imagined a metanovel and now the metanovel exists—or at least it is beginning to exist,
getting realer by the day. Imagination is leading to existence. Decision, imagination and practice,
practice, practice, help me create, create, create what I really want to create.
Imagination itself takes practice, and knowing what I want to imagine takes practice, too.
The more I practice imagining and then practice creating what I imagine the more what I ima­
gine approaches what I imagine I actually want.
Once I found I was writing a metanovel and not just a novel, I imagined what I wanted to
create, and I created what I had imagined. But it wasn’t a metanovel. I’d only imagined that I
had imagined one. I’d really imagined a metanovel draft. And that’s what I’d created.
I was happy with my metanovel draft, but I wanted something more than that. So I imagined
that I could improve it. And I improved it by editing it. And I was happy with the improvement.
But what I’d imagined was still not a metanovel. It was an improved metanovel draft, but still
not a metanovel. I didn’t have enough practice imagining a metanovel to actually imagine one.
So I practiced some more. Imagining a metanovel takes more work than imagining a metan­
ovel draft or an improved metanovel draft. And creating a metanovel takes more work than cre­
ating a metanovel draft. That was clear now.
A metanovel requires a metanovel draft to start with, followed by a great deal of repeated
imagining, deciding, practicing, and working to make it better. Each time that I imagined that I
had imagined a metanovel, I imagined something that was more like the metanovel I wanted—
yet still not that metanovel. My current metanovel draft, the one I am writing right now, isn’t
quite what I want, but it’s on its way. And the one you’re reading isn’t quite there either. But it’s
close enough. It’s really very close. And it’s evolving.
Evolving!

Interchapter Report 1: Success In The Interchapter
Break
Time in the metauniverse is not linear. It is bidirectional, self-referential, recursive, chaotic
and fractal. Streams of time branch off, loop back and curve around. They disappear when they
are deleted.
Time during interchapter breaks can flow in any direction and at any speed. A day might
pass between two chapters. Or a minute, a week, or ten years. Time might move backward
between a narrative chapter and a flashback. If might jump forward to a flash forward. Time
might stop completely while the author went to the pee, as the author just did.
To prove his point, between two earlier chapters, the author was able to write a brilliant first
draft; edit it and polish it to perfection; send it off to a publisher; get it accepted; get a huge ad­
vance; and sell the movie rights.
His book got rave reviews; it went straight to the top of the New York Times best-sellers list;
it stayed there for nearly a year. He was invited to all the popular talk shows; he bantered wittily
with the hosts and hostesses; he became celebrity; he had more than 10,000,000 followers on
Twitter and Google+. He refused to have anything to do with Facebook. Mark Zuckerburg called
him constantly, pleading for him to join. He told Mark to get stuffed.
Life changed for him.
He spent the last ten years of his life in the interchapter break writing the story of his life. He
included all four hundred pages in the metanovel. It was exciting reading. But just before he died
in the interchapter break, a fulfilled and happy man at the age of 92, he realized that most of
what happened in the break was a distraction. He cut almost all of it and pasted it into a book
he’ll give his kids some day.
All that was left was this report of his success.

Interchapter Report 2: Metapractice 101
It took him years in an interchapter break to practice lying until he could do it skillfully. In
the beginning he didn’t even know how to practice. Before he could practice lying, he needed to
spend a year practice practicing. But how could he learn to metapractice?
He’d developed powerful beliefs about practice. He had believed that he didn’t need to prac­
tice. When he realized that he did need to practice, he replaced that wrong belief with other
wrong beliefs: that he didn’t have the ability to practice; that he did not have the discipline to
practice; that he couldn’t learn to practice; that he couldn’t develop self-discipline. He used these
beliefs to support his belief that the situation was hopeless.
But he did have one very odd belief that led him to success. He believed, for some reason,
that he should reread page 105 carefully. He did, and remembered what he’d written earlier
about The Most Powerful Forces In The Universe.
He remembered that belief was the most powerful force in the universe. He remembered that
his beliefs were not helping him. He remembered that he could change his beliefs. He re­
membered that it could be difficult. By the time he’d finished writing this paragraph he had de­
cided to change his beliefs.
He imagined some new, more helpful beliefs. He decided to believe there was hope. He de­
cided to believe that he did have the ability to practice and the discipline to practice and the dis­
cipline and ability to learn. For two minutes he tried to imagine practicing in a disciplined way,
then stopped, exhausted. He realized that those beliefs were welded into a metabelief system.
A metabelief system is a set of beliefs along with a set of beliefs about beliefs that support
and reinforce one another. While you try to change a belief, the metabeliefs resist change and
change the new belief back. His metabelief system had two powerful metabeliefs: he believed
that his beliefs were true, and be believed that those beliefs could not be changed.
He decided to change his metabeliefs. And he succeeded, but only because his metameta­
beliefs—that his metabeliefs were true and unchangeable—were weak. If they’d been stronger,
he’d have had to go meta at least one more level.
Changing his metametabliefs was easy. Changing his metabeliefs was a bit harder. Once
he’d changed them, he set to changing his beliefs. Then he set to imagining a more effective ap­
proach. He couldn’t imagine that he could learn to practice, but he could imagine that he could
imagine it. So he metaimagined that he could learn to practice.
It was hard, but he found that he could do it. He practiced until he could easily imagine that
he could imagine it. Then he metaimagined that he had self-discipline despite imagined distrac­
tions, self-doubt, and opposition. That took more work, but he did it. Then he metaimagined self-
discipline despite greater imagined obstacles. Each time he tried it became easier and each time
it became easier he did it again, only with still greater imagined obstacles. In a short span of in­
terchapter time he was able to metaimagine self-discipline while a hundred thousand imagined
voices screamed that he could not do it.

Finally he was ready to imagine. He took a deep breath and imagined that he had the self-

Interchapter Report 3: Lying 101
And now he was ready to do what he’d set out to do: to practice lying so he could become a
skilled liar. He started by imaging success: that he was a really good liar. He burst into laughter
immediately. The idea was ludicrous. He repeated imaging he was skilled until he stopped laugh­
ing, then stopped giggling, then until he could imagine it with confidence. Then he imagined
practicing lying. It took a while for him to image it, but he did. Finally he was ready to imagine
lying
He started by imagining telling innocent lies to naive children; later small lies to older chil­
dren; then small lies to oblivious adults. He worked his way up to telling whoppers to steely-
eyed cynics sensitive to the slightest evidence of duplicity. And when he was good at that, he
practiced telling the muse that he’d destroyed his work, then imagined dealing effectively with
her possible reactions and responses.
The more he practiced lying, the better a liar he knew he was becoming.
Finally he was ready to face the music and face the muse and lie, right to her face.
