The chapter changed, of course. But that was one small detail. The big change was my per­
ception of time. Like everyone else, I had spent my life stuck in a single moment of time at a
time. The time at any time was called ‘the present.’ The times yet to come were called ‘the fu­
ture.’ And the times that I had already experienced ‘the past.’ Like everyone and everything else
I was dragged, helpless, from past times through future times through the present moment.
But when God snapped her fingers I was no longer stuck in one moment of time at a time. I
was outside of time. I could see in four dimensions: three spatial directions plus time.
“Like a Tralfamadorean,” said the metanovel!”
“What’s that?” asked Mom.
“The Tralfamadoreans were fictional characters created by a science fiction author
named Kurt Vonnegut,” I said.
“Oh I know Kurt,” said Mom. “He’s dead, isn’t he.”
“Yes,” I said. “He died in 2007.”
“He was 84,” Mom said. “Same age as your Dad. I beat them both by ten years. Not
that I’m keeping score. But I did win.”
“If you know Kurt you’ll want to read Literary Near-Immortality, on page 58,” said
the metanovel to my Mom. “And also Reality And The Flow Of Time. It keeps moving
around, but it’s on page 69 this time.
I wasn’t listening. I was outside of time. I looked at the chair in front of me. I could see it
in the usual way and I could see it in a new way. I could see it moving through space and
through time, from place to place and time to time in my office; further back I could see it move
from a store to my office, from a factory to the store, see it being assembled in the factory, then
see its parts, still earlier in time, converging on the factory.
I looked at my hand. I could see it in the present and see where it had been and how it had
moved in the past. I could see it shrinking, becoming a baby hand, then shrinking to become an
fetal flipper, an embryonic limb bud, a cell in a gastrula, a cell in a blastula, a single celled zy­
gote; then further back I saw it split, and became a sperm and an egg, and further back the sperm
became a part of my father’s body, the egg became a part of my mother’s, then further back my
father and mother each changed to children, to embryos, to zygotes, to sperms and eggs.
I could see individuals in past generations change from adults to zygotes, then split and be­
come gametes and join the bodies of the parents that had created them. Evolution reversed itself
and I could see parents who were barely human, then no longer human, then no longer primates,
then no longer mammals; I could see back further to multicelled creatures, then single-celled
creatures, then living things simpler even than a single cell.
I could see the entire tree of life: me and all of my ancestors, back to an early replicator that
was so simple it could not compete with living things today. And I could see all of my ancestors’
descendants, which included every creature on earth.

“Is that what you mean?” I asked God. “That I am related to every human on the planet and
to every living thing?”
“I’m just showing you my greatest invention in four dimensions,” said God. “You’ve known
the facts of evolution. But like your limited understanding of Big Bang theory, you haven’t real­
ized its implications. Evolution isn’t a just-so story about how species came to exist; it’s a
mechanism that reveals your relationship to life.”
God was right. I wasn’t isolated. My family was all around me, and it was huge! It covered
the earth, from the highest mountains to the driest deserts; some of my relatives lived miles un­
dersea, near 400 degree thermal vents. Some lived in the freezing arctic. Some of my relatives
were whales and some had been dinosaurs. My body was filled with distant family members: in­
testinal flora and fauna, symbionts, saprophytes, and parasites, helping me, living with me, and
living off me. On every surface tiny relatives lived; the woods were full of distant relations—
trees, moss, birds, insects. My relatives—seaweed, fish, and crabs among them—filled the Salt
Pond outside my house; relatives floated in the clear autumn air, some as big as birds, and some
smaller than dust.
My family was everywhere.
I’d seen myself as an isolated individual connected to a few friends, to my wife and daugh­
ters, brother and sister, and through them to other family members and other acquaintances. I
had seen myself living in a small town in Maine, weakly connected to the distant, bustling cen­
ters of civilization. Now I saw myself as God wanted me to see myself: the at the center of Her
universe; and product of Her evolution, a family member connected to the rest of my family—
every creature that lived, that had lived and that would ever lived on earth.
At the center of the universe. A mistake; Surrounded by family!
The geniuses of history were all family members; every artist and every musician was a
family member. Every brilliant innovation over the entire history of humanity came from one of
my relatives, all the result of mistakes, and all made by mistakes.
There were black sheep in the family, too, both literally and figuratively. Adolph Hitler was
part of my family. So was Jean Kambana, former prime minister of Rwanda, who helped organ­
ize the slaughter of a nearly a million Tutsi, and Pol Pot, responsible for 2.5 million deaths, and
Slobodan Milošević responsible for a mere few hundred thousand.
The literal and figurative black sheep were a minority of my relatives because so few
of my relatives were mammals. Most of my relatives were hard working, some of them
working in factories, some in offices, some of them visiting flowers for nectar and mak­
ing honey, and others turning milk into cheese.
I looked around. No matter what happened to me I was always at the center of the universe
and I was always surrounded by my family! And I was always a mistake and an accident!
Even if things were going badly for me, even if I was having trouble enjoying what was hap­
pening to me, other family members were prospering, creating, expanding. All I had to do was
look around me, from the center of the universe and connect to my family. I was never alone. All
I had to do was stop taking myself so seriously and enjoy the pleasure of others. There was al­
ways something I could enjoy.
